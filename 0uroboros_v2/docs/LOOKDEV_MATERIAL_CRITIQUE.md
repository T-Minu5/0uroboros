## Six actionable findings

1. **Make the chassis readable through broad reflections.** The baseline has near-black broad surfaces with narrow blue edges, so large forms read as flat cutouts. Current source already has an environment and distinct metalness/roughness values: adding another generic environment is not the answer. Shape the existing neutral light reflection so it crosses front bevels and corner shoulders; keep cyan/pink reflections narrower. Aim for recognizable housing planes in a desaturated screenshot before adding more glow. This follows the supplied housing concepts and the material separation visible in the Three.js example.

2. **Separate three material families.** Use machined blue-gray metal on perimeter armor, darker rough polymer on separators, and quiet coated/glass surfaces inside wells. Add subtle original directional microfinish and sparse wear primarily through roughness/normal variation. Avoid uniform noise across every surface or scratches that compete with cards. Judge from the gameplay camera: texture that is only visible in a close-up cannot carry the whole result. PBR maps require appropriate color-space handling; roughness/normal data should remain non-color data, as documented by Three.js.

3. **Make layered geometry visibly touch.** Baseline peripheral pieces cast separated rectangular shadows onto a gray ground, producing a scattered model-kit impression. New armor needs overlapping lips, shadowed seams and bevel catches at three scales: chassis mass, corner/rail assembly, then a few fasteners. Soften the broad ground shadow while preserving short contact darkening between adjoining pieces. If adding AO, examine AO-only output and keep it local; black halos around every component would merely replace one artificial cue with another. The GTAO example offers useful debug views, not a mandate to add expensive postprocessing.

4. **Contain light inside the machine.** The baseline pink perimeter reads like a uniformly bright outline tube; all four DC banks present near-white cyan fields with repeated high-frequency waveforms. That pulls attention away from the hand and Nodes even at idle. Break the rim with housings/clamps, recess the emitter and make spill strongest on nearby metal. Give DCs dark endcaps, a restrained illuminated body and a narrow bright core. Preserve enough contrast for an impact to visibly increase brightness. The supplied concepts support this hierarchy without importing their exact forms.

5. **Give the table an environment without reducing play space.** The flat gray surroundings offer little scale or grounding. Use a very restrained dark backdrop and soft falloff below/behind the chassis, with the board staying distinctly lighter than its immediate surround. Preserve the present camera and gameplay framing first; avoid bringing dramatic background props into the wells or shrinking the board. Snap's observed edge framing and the supplied concept's layered perimeter support depth at the margins while keeping the center available for play.

6. **Reserve the highest contrast for game state.** A still frame should read hand/card identity, score and current source before decorative banks. During an event, concentrate highlights on the actual source, traveled path and destination; idle machinery should be quieter. Hearthstone's inspected transition graphic demonstrates foreground separation, while Snap screenshots demonstrate local action focus; neither provides measured timing evidence. Keep the approved Node plates and their text unchanged during this material pass.

## First in-game pass review

Actually compared `docs/evidence/board-lookdev-before.png` with `board-lookdev-pass1.png`. These are different dealt states, so this is a material/composition comparison rather than a pixel-diff validation. Clear improvements: DC illumination is controlled rather than near-white noise; corner and rail assemblies visibly connect; front extrusion adds thickness. The center Node plates remain readable.

Three highest-impact next changes:

1. **Separate glass from metal by value.** Well faces and rail shoulders now share a similar medium blue-gray value. Darken smoked well glass and rough polymer separators substantially; reserve broad cool reflections for metal shoulders/bevels. Increasing engraving contrast alone will not solve the shared material appearance.
2. **Shape the two outer cyan highlights.** The bright spots above the outer wells still look like circular painted blooms. Prefer elongated reflection/spill aligned to a bevel or recessed channel, with less bright coverage across the flat well face. Keep the quieter DC treatment achieved in pass 1.
3. **Shorten grounding shadows.** Hard rightward silhouettes, particularly the new corner/rail teeth, still suggest independent extruded pieces placed on a flat stage. Remove long hard stage projection while preserving small seam shadows and soft local contact grounding. Do not erase all depth cues together.

## Second integrated review

Inspected fresh `docs/evidence/runtime-initial.png` and `docs/evidence/drain-in-play.png` after the second pass. Dark well glass now separates from perimeter shoulders; etching is visible but subordinate. DC grids are controlled and readable, cyan reflection is shaped along edges rather than circular hotspots, and long stage silhouettes no longer dominate grounding. No new material/readability regression is evident in these two captures. The approved central plates retain their hierarchy. The active violet wave rings cross both the Node label and score in the populated capture: keep this opacity at or below its current level rather than increasing bloom. No further feature expansion is recommended from these stills.

Read-only code check of `src/BoardAtmosphere.tsx` found a shader portability issue: its ground-spill `pow` calls receive negative bases over parts of the plane. GLSL `pow` is undefined for negative bases even when the exponent is an integer value. Use `abs(base)` before `pow`, or multiplication for square terms. This was reported to the implementation owner; no production edit was made here. Ordinary-unmount disposal of the composer and owned passes is explicitly present; no obvious disposal leak was identified from the limited code review. GPU behavior and repeated-mount lifecycle were not independently exercised.
